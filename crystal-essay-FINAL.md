# Crystal's Personal Statement: FINAL (her own words)

According to my seven-year-old self, I'm going to hell for three words.

Every Friday night, my family had dinner at a family friend's house, where I went straight for the wife's LA galbi, Korean short ribs marinated in her secret Coca-Cola recipe. One of their grown sons had a tattoo going down his arm. I'd been taught that people with tattoos were sinners. At every dinner, I kept my distance. I'd crouch beside my mom's chair and peek through the space where her arm met her rib cage. I'd stare as I chewed off every last bit of meat from the rib, judging not only his tattoo, but him.

Scrolling late one night, I stopped at a photo of a girl with long black hair down to her waist, like mine, and a fine-line word on her collarbone. I couldn't make out what it said. That's what I liked about it: only she knew. I saw myself in her, as if she carried herself the way I wanted to carry myself. I felt guilty. If she was a sinner too, why was she different from the man at dinner? I froze, staring at the screen, torn between judging her and admiring her confidence in doing something for herself, not for the judgment of others. Then I liked the photo.

In Montreal for my 17th birthday, my mom gave me a tattoo: "Honesty is Faith," on my lower abdomen. Three words my seven-year-old self would have damned me for, and one of them is Faith. My mom stood to my left, watching each word pierce my skin, something she'd never do herself. She didn't ask me to explain them. Every time I look down, I remember that she didn't need to see the world the way I do to hold my shirt up for me and help me decide what font I wanted. After we left the hole-in-the-wall tattoo shop, we ran into the nearest café to escape the piercing wind and powdery snow. I tried to explain to her what it meant. I couldn't tell if I was stumbling over the words because my teeth were chattering, or because I didn't know what to say. When I'm asked what my tattoo says, I stutter, toggling back-and-forth between whether I want to attempt to explain it or say "It's just something I always tell myself." I always land on the second option.

The man at those Friday dinners never owed me an explanation for his tattoo. I don't owe anyone one for mine. One night, eyes closed in bed, my hands half-folded, I couldn't get past "Dear God, thank you for this day." I kept repeating "I'm sorry" until my finger grazed my tattoo, reminding me that admitting my doubt was what made my faith mine.

My APCSP teacher had a tattoo on her back. As I stared at it in class, I wondered what it meant to her. It suited her; she was whimsical and she giggled every time she titled a Google Classroom assignment with an absurd amount of emojis. I still don't know what it means, and it's important that I don't. After winter recess, I walked into class with my new tattoo, and she noticed it. She didn't ask what it meant, and I felt relieved. She understood.

For years, I'd lie awake at 3:17 a.m., hearing the same car racing down the freeway behind my house, resenting that if I were asked "Who are you?" I wouldn't have a confident answer. Now, I answer "I don't know" and fall asleep before I can even hear that car. Just as I changed my mind about tattoos, I could wake up in a panic one day with a fully developed frontal lobe, trying to scrub off my tattoo, thinking "This isn't what I believe anymore." But I'm content with not knowing yet.
