# Crystal's Personal Statement: FINAL (her own words)

I was seven when I decided a black wave could send a man to hell.

Every Friday night, my family had dinner at a family friend's house. One of their grown sons had a tattoo going down his arm. I'd been taught that people with tattoos were sinners. At every dinner, I kept my distance. I'd crouch beside my mom's chair and peek through the space where her arm met her rib cage. I'd stare at his tattoo, judging not only it, but him as I decided he was a bad person.

Scrolling late one night, I stopped at a photo of a girl with long black hair down to her waist, like mine, and a fine-line word on her collarbone. I couldn't make out what it said. That's what I liked about it: only she knew. I saw myself in her, as if she carried herself the way I wanted to carry myself. I just hadn't known it until then. I admired the confidence of doing something for yourself and not for the judgment of others.

In Montreal for my 17th birthday, my mom gave me a tattoo: "Honesty is Faith," on my lower abdomen. After we left the hole-in-the-wall tattoo shop, we ran into the nearest café to escape the piercing wind and powdery snow. I tried to explain to her what it meant. I couldn't tell if I was stumbling over the words because my teeth were chattering, or because I didn't know what to say. When I'm asked what my tattoo says, I stutter, toggling back-and-forth between whether or not I want to attempt to explain it or just say "It's just something that I always tell myself." I always land on the second option.

The man at those Friday dinners never owed me an explanation for his tattoo. I don't owe anyone one for mine. On nights when I tossed and turned, struggling to find the words to confess my guilt for questioning my faith, "Honesty is Faith" became my prayer. On nights when I sat on the bathroom floor sobbing, my face buried in my mom's leg, and told her "I don't know what's wrong" instead of explaining what really kept me up at night, it read as a reminder to stay honest with those around me and with myself.

Now I actively seek out tattoos on people. My APCSP teacher had one on her back. As I stared at it in class, I wondered what it meant to her. It made sense to me that she had it; she was whimsical and let out a little giggle every time she titled a Google Classroom assignment with an absurd amount of emojis. But I still don't know what her tattoo means, and it's important that I don't. After winter recess, I walked into class with my new tattoo, and she noticed it. She didn't ask what it meant, and I felt relieved. She understood.

My mom stood to my left, watching each word pierce my skin, something she'd never do herself. She didn't ask me to explain them. Every time I look down, I remember that she didn't need to see the world the way I do to hold my shirt up for me and help me decide what font I wanted.

On those nights I struggled to tell my mom about, I'd lie awake at 3:17 a.m., hearing the same two cars racing down the freeway behind my house, resenting the fact that if I were asked "Who are you?" I wouldn't have a confident answer. But where I stand in the world isn't something that I want to master anymore. Just like I changed my mind about tattoos over the years, I could wake up in ten years with a fully developed frontal lobe and think "This just isn't what I believe anymore." I'm okay with not knowing whether I will.
